---
title: Why Early Engineering Mistakes Matter
categories: [Engineering, Reflection]
tags: [engineering, learning, problem-solving]
---

Nobody starts by running fast.

First we learn to stand.

Then we learn to walk.

Then we fall, stand up again, and try once more.

Engineering experience is not very different.

Earlier in my career, I learned this in an uncomfortable way.

Like many young engineers, I wanted to solve problems quickly.

I wanted to show that I understood the task.

I wanted to make progress.

And sometimes that meant moving too fast.

The mistake was not always a wrong calculation or a bad technical idea.

Sometimes the real mistake was accepting the first understanding of the problem too easily.

What did I assume?

What did I not ask?

What constraint did I notice too late?

Who would be affected by the decision later?

These questions are not always obvious at the beginning.

They often become clear only after something does not go as planned.

That is why early mistakes can be useful.

Not because mistakes are good by themselves.

But because they can change how we think.

They can teach us to slow down before choosing a direction.

They can teach us to check assumptions.

They can teach us to ask better questions.

They can teach us to look beyond the part and understand the process around it.

Today, I try to treat simple-looking tasks with more respect.

A small bracket, a moved hole, a changed tolerance, or a quick drawing update can still have consequences.

Not because every task is complicated.

But because every task has a context.

Manufacturing has a context.

Assembly has a context.

Inspection has a context.

Maintenance has a context.

People have a context.

Early engineering mistakes are uncomfortable.

But if they improve judgement, they are not wasted.

The goal is not to avoid every fall.

The goal is to stand up with better questions, better habits, and better engineering judgement.
