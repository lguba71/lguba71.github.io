---
title: Why Tool Access Matters in Mechanical Design
categories: [Engineering, Design]

---

A CAD model can look correct and still be difficult to manufacture, assemble, inspect, or maintain.

One common reason is simple:

the tool cannot reach the feature properly.

Tool access is easy to overlook during design because CAD does not always show the real working situation.

A hole may be in the right position, but too close to a wall for a drill, reamer, or inspection probe.

A screw may be correctly selected, but placed where a hex key, socket, or torque wrench cannot be used comfortably.

A pocket may look clean in the model, but require a long, unstable cutter to machine.

A sensor, cover, or bracket may fit into the assembly, but later become difficult to remove during maintenance.

The geometry may be correct.

The design may still create unnecessary work.

Tool access is not only a machining issue.

It also affects assembly, tightening, inspection, adjustment, cleaning, and future service.

A design decision that saves a few millimetres in CAD can later cost time on the shop floor.

It can increase machining time.

It can make assembly uncomfortable.

It can require special tools.

It can make inspection less reliable.

It can turn a simple maintenance task into a frustrating operation.

![Why tool access matters in mechanical design](/assets/img/posts/why-tool-access-matters-in-mechanical-design.png)

Before finalizing a design, I like to check:

- can the machining tool reach the feature with a stable setup?
- is there enough space for standard assembly tools?
- can fasteners be tightened in the intended direction?
- can critical features be inspected clearly?
- can parts be adjusted or removed later if needed?
- does the design require special tools without a good reason?
- would the operator, supplier, or maintenance technician understand the intended access?

Good mechanical design is not only about placing features correctly.

It is also about making them reachable.

A practical design considers the part, the tool, the hand, the measuring device, and the real working environment.

If the tool cannot reach the feature, the feature is not really practical.

This is why tool access should be considered early, not discovered late during manufacturing or assembly.
