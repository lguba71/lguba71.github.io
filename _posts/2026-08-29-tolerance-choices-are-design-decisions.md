Tolerances are often treated as drawing details.

Something to define near the end of the design process.

But tolerance choices are not only documentation work.

They are design decisions.

A tolerance defines how much variation is acceptable.

That variation affects manufacturing, inspection, assembly, cost, supplier communication, and product reliability.

A tight tolerance may look safe on a drawing.

But it can make a part more expensive to manufacture.

It can require a different machining process.

It can increase inspection time.

It can reduce supplier flexibility.

It can also create unnecessary rejected parts if the tolerance is tighter than the function really needs.

On the other hand, a tolerance that is too loose can create real problems.

Parts may not fit together correctly.

Assembly may become unstable or inconsistent.

Adjustment may take longer.

Noise, vibration, wear, or alignment problems may appear later.

The important question is not whether a tolerance is tight or loose.

The important question is whether it matches the function.

Before defining a tolerance, I like to ask:

- what function does this dimension control?
- what happens if the part is at the upper limit?
- what happens if the part is at the lower limit?
- does this tolerance affect assembly, alignment, sealing, movement, or safety?
- can the supplier manufacture it with a stable process?
- can it be inspected clearly and economically?
- is the tolerance based on function, or only on habit?

Good tolerancing is not about making every dimension precise.

It is about making the right dimensions precise enough.

A useful tolerance protects the function of the design without creating unnecessary manufacturing cost.

This is why tolerance choices should be part of design thinking, not only drawing cleanup.

They connect engineering intent with real manufacturing capability.
